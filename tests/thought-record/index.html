Thought Record

A thought record is one of the oldest and best-tested tools in cognitive-behavioral therapy: a structured way of writing down what happened, what you felt, what you thought, and what the evidence actually says, so that a reaction can be examined instead of obeyed. This version runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you write is stored or sent anywhere, and when you finish you can download your record as a plain text file that is yours alone.

The Tool

Separate facts from interpretation

Seven steps, one at a time. Each step shows a worked example (a manager, a phone, a presentation) so you can see what the step asks for before you write your own.

The situation

What happened? Stick to what a camera would have recorded: who, what, where, when. No interpretation yet.

Example

Tuesday, 4:40 pm. I finished presenting the quarterly numbers. My manager looked at her phone twice during the last five minutes and left the room without commenting.

The emotion and its intensity

Name the emotion in one word and rate how strong it is right now, from 0 (not at all) to 10 (the most intense you can imagine).

5
0 · not at all10 · the most intense
Example

Ashamed, 7.

The automatic thought

What went through your mind in that moment? Write the thought exactly as it appeared, as a full sentence.

Example

She thinks the presentation was a waste of her time, and she has already decided I am not ready for the promotion.

Does your thought wear one of these disguises? Optional

Tick any that fit. This step is optional, but what you mark here sharpens the help in step 5 and travels into your downloaded record.

Example

Mind reading + fortune telling: the thought claims to know what she thinks now and what she will decide later.

Evidence for the thought

What facts support the thought? Facts only: the kind you could show someone else.

Example

She looked at her phone twice. She left without commenting. Last month she said the deck needed tightening.

Evidence against the thought

What facts do not fit the thought? What would you notice if you were not already convinced?

Example

She has looked at her phone in every meeting this quarter. She left at 4:45 for a call that was on the shared calendar. She approved the numbers by email that morning. Nobody has said a word about the promotion, in either direction.

The balanced thought

Write a thought that holds all the evidence at once. Not a positive thought: an accurate one.

Use the formula if it helps

It is true that X, and also true that Y.

Example

It is true that I do not know what she thought of the presentation, and also true that the evidence points to a busy manager with a call to make, not a verdict on my career. If I want to know, I can ask her.

Rate the emotion again

Same emotion, same scale, 0 to 10. Answer honestly, not hopefully.

5
0 · not at all10 · the most intense
Example

Ashamed: from 7 to 4.

Your record

Read it once, slowly. Then download it if you want to keep it: the file is created on your device and nowhere else.

This is the practical form of a distinction explored in depth in The Baseline Psychotherapy Protocol: separating data from interpretation.

Nothing you write here leaves your device or is stored: the record disappears when you close the page. The download is a plain text file saved only where you choose.

Thought Record FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions About
the Thought Record

A thought record is a structured worksheet from cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), developed in the tradition of Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy and refined over decades of clinical use. It walks you through a situation, the emotion it produced, the automatic thought behind it, the evidence on both sides, and a more balanced thought. This version follows that classic structure; the tool is not an invention of this practice.

Yes, for many people it does, and self-guided thought records are a standard part of CBT homework. The skill improves with repetition: the first few feel slow and the balanced thought sounds forced. What self-practice cannot do is spot the pattern behind repeated records, which is where a clinician adds value.

That happens, and it is useful information. Sometimes the automatic thought you wrote is not the one carrying the charge, and a second record on a deeper thought helps. Sometimes the emotion fits the facts and the work is to tolerate it rather than argue with it. If emotions stay high across many records, that is a reason to bring them to a session.
