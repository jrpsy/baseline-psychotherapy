The Trait Meta-Mood Scale: measure your emotional intelligence

The Trait Meta-Mood Scale (TMMS) is the original self-report measure of perceived emotional intelligence, built by the researchers who introduced the concept of emotional intelligence (Salovey & Mayer, 1990) and published as a scale by Salovey, Mayer, Goldman, Turvey and Palfai (1995). Thirty statements, three dimensions: how much attention you pay to your feelings, how clearly you can tell them apart, and how well you repair a bad mood. Scored in your browser; nothing leaves your device.

The Scale

Take the Trait Meta-Mood Scale

Read each statement and choose the answer that best describes how you generally feel. There are no right or wrong answers, and it is best not to spend long on any one item.

The original TMMS does not define clinical cutoffs; the readings below are interpretive bands based on your average response per dimension, not diagnostic categories.

Attention below 2.5 means the statements about valuing and noticing feelings mostly got low agreement. Feelings carry information you may be skipping: the emotional meter on this site is a two-minute way to practice taking a reading.

Attention between 2.5 and 3.5 is the balanced range of the scale: you notice your feelings without being run by them.

Attention above 3.5 is a strong focus on feelings. In this scale, very high attention is not automatically a strength: research on the TMMS links it to rumination when clarity and repair are low. Read it together with the other two dimensions.

Clarity below 2.5 means feelings are often confusing or hard to name. Clarity is trainable: the thought record on this site separates what happened from what you felt and what you concluded.

Clarity between 2.5 and 3.5 means you can usually tell what you feel, with some blur under pressure.

Clarity above 3.5 means you can usually name and distinguish your feelings, which is the base that regulation builds on.

Repair below 2.5 means bad moods tend to stay. This is exactly what structured emotional regulation work develops, and the most workable of the three dimensions.

Repair between 2.5 and 3.5 means you can move a bad mood some of the time; the skill is present and can be made more reliable.

Repair above 3.5 means you can usually move a bad mood along. Protect what already works for you.

Your result is a starting point, not a verdict. If you want to make sense of it with a professional, the first conversation is free: 15 minutes, honest, no commitment.

What Your Averages Mean

Each dimension is the mean of its items on the 1 to 5 scale, after reversing the statements worded in the negative. The bands are anchored to the scale itself, not to population norms.

The TMMS is a self-report measure of perceived emotional intelligence, not a diagnosis or an ability test. The 30 statements are reproduced from the original English scale of Salovey, Mayer, Goldman, Turvey and Palfai (1995): 13 items measure attention (8 reverse-scored), 11 measure clarity (5 reverse-scored) and 6 measure repair (2 reverse-scored). The abbreviated Spanish adaptation with validated cutoffs, the TMMS-24, is also free on this site. Your answers are processed entirely in your browser: nothing is sent, stored, or seen by anyone but you.

Frequently Asked Questions

The Trait Meta-Mood Scale (TMMS) measures perceived emotional intelligence: how much attention you pay to your feelings, how clearly you can identify them, and how well you repair a negative mood. It was built by Peter Salovey and John Mayer, the researchers who introduced the concept of emotional intelligence in 1990, and published with colleagues in 1995. This page reproduces the 30-item version of the original English scale.

The TMMS-24 is the abbreviated Spanish adaptation, also free on this site. Fernández-Berrocal, Extremera and Ramos (2004) shortened the original scale to 24 items, reworded the negatively phrased statements in positive form and published sex-specific cutoffs validated in Spanish. This page keeps the 30 original English items, scores the reverse-keyed statements as the authors intended, and uses transparent interpretive bands instead of cutoffs, because the original scale does not define them.

Each dimension is the average of your answers on a 1 to 5 scale, after reversing the items worded in the negative. Clarity and repair read in the expected direction: higher tends to go with better regulation. Attention does not: a very high score can reflect excessive monitoring of feelings rather than a strength, especially when clarity and repair are low. The most useful reading is the profile of the three together.
