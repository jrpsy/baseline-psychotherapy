Expat Depression: Signs, Causes,
and What Actually Helps

By J.R. Hernandez, Psychotherapist · October 5, 2026 · 10 min read

You have the life you chose. The job that justified the move, the apartment, the city you once showed friends on a map. You still deliver at work, still answer "really well" when someone asks how it is going. And somewhere in the last few months the texture went out of it: the restaurant you loved tastes like nothing, the weekend is something to get through, and you cannot remember the last time you looked forward to anything.

This is not a verdict on the decision to move. It is depression, presenting the way it most often presents in people who function: not as sadness but as flatness, not as collapse but as a slow withdrawal from everything that is not required.

There is no separate illness called expat depression. Depression is the same condition on every side of a border, and it responds to the same treatments. What relocation changes are the conditions around it, and the way it gets misread. As a psychotherapist specialized in mood disorders who has lived across six countries on three continents, I see depression abroad misattributed to adjustment, tiredness, or "not having clicked with the country" more often than I see it named.

The six signs of expat depression, in brief: pleasure goes before performance does; the dominant feeling is flatness, not sadness; you let calls from home ring out; the country becomes the explanation for everything; sleep, appetite, and energy shift without a clear reason; and you have started to doubt the decision itself rather than any particular problem. Each sign is explained in detail below, followed by what clinically helps.

What Makes Expat Depression Different

Clinically, nothing. The World Health Organization estimates that around 280 million people live with depression, and the core picture is the same everywhere: persistent low mood or loss of interest, with changes in energy, sleep, appetite, concentration, and self-regard, lasting more than two weeks. A 2018 meta-analysis of 25 studies found a pooled depression prevalence of 15.6% among international migrants, with employment, education, and length of residence shifting the figure substantially. Those studies mixed very different populations, so it is not an expat rate; what it establishes is that this is common, and that circumstances matter more than the move itself.

What differs abroad is the context, and it differs in a specific way: several protective conditions change at once. The social network that would have noticed you going quiet is on a screen. The language that carried nuance now carries effort. Professional identity, legal certainty, and the ordinary scaffolding of a week can all be in transition at the same time.

And the loss is unacknowledged, because you chose it. Grief for a life you left voluntarily does not get named as grief, by you or by anyone around you. That is the difference that matters: not a different illness, but a loss nobody counts, in a context with fewer people to count it.

It also travels with its neighbors. When the dominant experience is depletion from relentless demand, the picture is closer to expat burnout; when it is activation and vigilance, closer to expat anxiety. Depression is distinguished by what it takes away: interest, pleasure, and the sense that anything is worth the effort.

Why Depression Abroad Hides Behind Competence

Work holds. Deadlines, other people's expectations, and financial necessity supply a structure that keeps performance intact long after motivation, pleasure, and social life have gone. From the outside, the visible part looks fine. The parts that deteriorate first are the ones nobody at work can see.

This is why so many expats recognize the same sentence: I can present to forty people and then cannot make myself reply to a friend. The presentation requires competence, which is intact. The reply requires wanting something, which is what has gone. Add a good salary, an enviable city, and a life other people describe as a dream, and the result is a specific guilt: the sense that you have no right to be struggling. The guilt does not protect you from the condition. It only stops you mentioning it.

Why "Give It Time" Is Not a Clinical Strategy

Ordinary adjustment does resolve with time: the environment becomes familiar, routines form, and mood follows. Depression does not, because it runs on a loop that time feeds rather than breaks. Less motivation leads to less activity, less activity to fewer rewarding experiences, fewer rewards to less motivation. Each month the loop runs, it narrows the life around it.

This is also why it so often starts late. The first months are structured and busy: a lease, a work permit, a city to learn. Novelty and logistics occupy attention. Then the tasks run out, and what remains is the actual shape of the life, with the losses now visible. Depression at month fourteen is not a failure to adjust. It is what happens when the scaffolding comes down.

Going home is not a treatment either. Repatriation can remove real stressors, but an established depressive episode usually travels with you, and home has changed while you were away. The decision deserves to be made from clarity, which is rarely available from inside the episode.

Six Signs That Low Mood Abroad Has Become Depression

1. Pleasure Goes Before Performance Does

The six signs that low mood abroad has become depression are: loss of pleasure that precedes any drop in performance, flatness rather than sadness as the dominant state, withdrawal from the people at home, the country becoming the explanation for everything, unexplained shifts in sleep, appetite, and energy, and doubt about the decision itself. The first is the most reliable. Things you moved here to enjoy no longer deliver anything, while your output at work is unchanged. Anhedonia, the loss of interest and pleasure, is a core feature of depression, not a side effect, and in high performers it is usually the first thing to go and the last to be noticed.

2. The Dominant Feeling Is Flatness, Not Sadness

Many people wait for sadness before they take depression seriously. Abroad, the common presentation is a turning-down of everything: irritability, exhaustion, a sense that the volume has been lowered on your own life. You do not cry. You just do not care, and you notice that you do not care. Not feeling sad rules nothing out.

3. You Let the Calls from Home Ring Out

A conversation with your mother, your oldest friend, your sibling, has become a task that costs energy you do not have. So you postpone it, then avoid it, then feel guilty about avoiding it, which makes the next call harder. The people who would have said "you do not sound like yourself" are the people you are now screening. Withdrawal removes your early warning system first.

4. The Country Becomes the Explanation for Everything

The weather, the people, the bureaucracy, the food. Real frustrations exist in every relocation, but depression changes how you evaluate them: when many unrelated areas of life feel unrewarding at once, the mind concludes that the common factor must be the place. The clinical question is whether the dissatisfaction is concentrated on one or two genuine problems, or whether almost everything has gone flat at the same time.

5. Sleep, Appetite, and Energy Shift Without a Clear Reason

Waking at four with nothing to think about, or sleeping ten hours and waking tired. Eating less without hunger, or more without appetite. A fatigue that rest does not touch. These are the somatic signatures of depression, and abroad they are reliably blamed on the climate, the time zone, or the work, long after those explanations have expired.

6. You Have Started to Doubt the Decision Itself

Not a particular problem, but the whole thing: "I made a mistake coming here," "I am not the kind of person who can do this." When hopelessness attaches to the decision rather than to any solvable situation, the depression has started to write the story. If moving abroad has also strained your relationship, the same withdrawal often shows up between partners, and each reads the other's flatness as a verdict.

The expat depression checklist

Seven quick checks, one line each, drawn from the six signs above. If several have been true for more than two weeks, low mood has most likely crossed into depression that needs to be treated as such.

  • Things you used to enjoy here no longer deliver anything, while your work output is unchanged.
  • The dominant feeling is flatness or irritability rather than sadness.
  • You have been letting calls and messages from home go unanswered.
  • Almost everything about this country has started to feel wrong at the same time.
  • Sleep, appetite, or energy have changed in ways the time zone no longer explains.
  • You have caught yourself thinking the move itself was the mistake.
  • You are still performing, and nobody around you knows any of this.

What Actually Helps: A Clinical Perspective

Depression is one of the most treatable conditions in clinical psychology, and the evidence is strong. Effective intervention abroad operates at three levels.

First, behavioral activation. Depression narrows life to work, home, sleep, repeat, and waiting for motivation to return before acting is the loop that keeps it there. Behavioral activation reverses the sequence: activity and reward are rebuilt systematically, in planned steps, so that motivation follows action rather than preceding it. Clinical guidelines rank it among the first-line psychological treatments, and when a life abroad has contracted to the essentials, it is the most directly relevant tool there is. It is the backbone of how I treat depression when there is no energy to begin.

Second, the cognitive work on the verdict. Depression presents its flatness as an assessment of the move, the country, and you. Treatment separates symptom from judgment: which of your conclusions would survive if your capacity for pleasure returned, and which are the depression speaking. Decisions about staying or leaving are deferred until that separation is clear.

Third, rebuilding the scaffolding. Social contact, routine, sleep, and movement are supports, not cures, but they make everything else work better. Exercise has stronger evidence than most people assume: a 2024 network meta-analysis of 218 trials found walking, strength training, and yoga among the approaches associated with meaningful symptom reduction. It is a support alongside treatment, not a replacement for it. Medication is an evidence-based option in appropriate cases, and that is a conversation with a physician, not an article.

Expat depression is not evidence that you chose wrong, or that you are not built for a life abroad. It is a treatable condition that happened while you were living abroad, and those are different sentences. It responds to structured clinical intervention when the loop that maintains it is interrupted. The goal is not to make you love the country. The goal is to return your capacity to feel anything about it, so the decision about where to live can be yours again.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is expat depression different from regular depression?

No. There is no separate diagnosis, and the symptoms, criteria, and effective treatments are the same wherever you live. What differs is the context: relocation can change your social support, language, professional identity, and legal certainty at the same time, and it removes the people who would normally notice the change in you. The loss also tends to go unacknowledged because the move was chosen. The treatment is standard; the assessment has to take the relocation into account, which is why a clinician who understands expat life shortens the work considerably.

Am I depressed, or do I just dislike this country?

Often both, and they feed each other. Genuine problems with a place exist and can contribute to depression. But once depression develops, it flattens the evaluation of everything, and the mind concludes that the common factor must be the country. Two questions separate them: is your dissatisfaction concentrated on one or two specific problems, or has almost everything gone flat at once; and do the things you used to enjoy still deliver something when you do them? If enjoyment itself has gone, the problem is broader than the place.

Will moving back home cure expat depression?

Not reliably. Repatriation can remove real stressors, and if isolation or work conditions are maintaining the episode, changing them matters. But an established depression usually travels with you, and returning brings its own losses: friends whose lives moved on, a home that exists partly in memory, and a version of you that has changed. The clinical advice is to treat first and decide second, so that the decision about where to live is made with your judgment intact rather than by the depression.

What treatment works for depression when you live abroad?

The same treatments that work anywhere: structured psychotherapy, in particular behavioral activation and cognitive behavioral therapy, medication in appropriate cases, and the two combined in more severe presentations, with sleep, routine, exercise, and social contact as supports. Online delivery works well for this, and it removes the barrier of finding a clinician in a language and system you may not yet navigate.
