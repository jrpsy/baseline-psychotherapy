Therapy in Singapore

How to Choose a Psychotherapist
in Singapore: A Clinical Guide

By J.R. Hernandez, Psychotherapist · October 8, 2026 · 10 min read

You have decided to see someone. You open Psychology Today, filter by Singapore, and find three hundred profiles with four different titles, a dozen acronyms after the names, fees from S$120 to S$400, and the same promise on almost every card: a safe space, a good connection, a journey. Nothing on the page tells you which of these people can actually treat what you have.

That is the problem this guide solves. Not which therapist is the nicest, but which one is a health professional with the training to identify the mechanism behind your symptoms and a method to change it. That distinction decides whether you get twelve sessions that resolve something or twelve months of pleasant conversation.

I write this as a psychotherapist who has worked with clients in Singapore and in more than 21 countries, trained in Medical Neuroscience at Duke University School of Medicine and specialized in anxiety and mood disorders with the American Psychological Association. I have also read a great many Singapore therapist profiles. What follows is what I would check if I were choosing for someone I cared about.

The five criteria, in brief: the title and what it legally means in Singapore; what the registration actually certifies; whether the clinician has a specialization and who granted it; whether the work is structured and measured or open-ended; and whether the therapist understands the body as well as the mind. Each is turned into a question you can ask below, followed by what a serious first session looks like.

What Is the Difference Between a Psychotherapist, a Psychologist, a Counsellor and a Psychiatrist in Singapore?

Four titles, four different scopes. A psychiatrist is a medical doctor registered with the Singapore Medical Council who diagnoses and prescribes; most do not deliver weekly psychotherapy. A psychologist holds a master's or doctorate in clinical or counselling psychology, is trained in psychological assessment and testing, and is registered with the Singapore Psychological Society; Singapore is moving toward statutory registration for the profession. A counsellor in Singapore typically holds a postgraduate qualification in counselling and may sit on the Singapore Association for Counselling's register after supervised practice; the work is often shorter-term and problem-focused.

A psychotherapist is defined by what the person does rather than by one degree: structured treatment of emotional and behavioral patterns using a defined method. In Singapore the title is not statutorily protected, which cuts both ways. It means anyone can use it, so the training behind it is what you must check. It also means the best psychotherapists are often those who combined several disciplines, because the title did not hand them a scope; their training did.

In practice, the title tells you the category of professional. It does not tell you whether this person knows why your sleep stopped restoring you, or what to do about it. For that you need to read the training, not the label.

What Does It Mean When a Therapist in Singapore Is Registered With the SAC or the SPS?

Registration with the SAC or the SPS certifies something real: a recognized postgraduate qualification, hundreds of supervised clinical hours, an ethics code, and continuing education. If a clinician is registered, you know they were trained and supervised. Confirm it. Then keep reading, because that is where most guides stop and where the real decision begins.

What registration does not certify is specialization. A registered counsellor may have no specific training in anxiety disorders; a registered psychologist may have spent their supervised hours on assessment rather than treatment. Nor does it certify any understanding of the physiology underneath the symptoms. Anxiety that will not switch off, sleep that does not restore, a resting heart rate that stays high: these are events in a nervous system before they are topics in a conversation. Training in medical neuroscience, in medicine, or in a specialization certified by a body like the American Psychological Association tells you the clinician works at that level. A register tells you they are safe to see. Those are different questions, and only the first one tells you whether this person can treat what you have.

For therapists trained abroad, which describes most who serve Singapore's international community, the same logic applies: look for a graduate degree, a named specialization and the institution that certified it, and a method that is written down somewhere you can read.

How Much Does the Relationship With Your Therapist Actually Matter?

Almost every guide to choosing a therapist in Singapore ends in the same place: what matters most is how you feel in the room. It is a comfortable sentence, and the research does not support it as stated. A client who came to me last year had spent eleven months with a counsellor she liked very much. She described the sessions as the best hour of her week. Her sleep, her Sunday dread, and the four a.m. waking were exactly where they had been in month one. Nobody had measured anything, so nobody had noticed.

The largest meta-analysis on the therapeutic alliance, by Flückiger, Del Re, Wampold and Horvath (2018), pooled 295 studies and found a correlation of 0.28 between alliance and outcome. That is a real, replicated effect, and it corresponds to roughly 8% of the variance in how people do. Connection is necessary. It is also, by the numbers, a minority of the result. The rest is carried by the method, by how well it fits the mechanism maintaining the problem, and by the competence of the clinician applying it: Wampold and Imel's analysis of therapist effects in The Great Psychotherapy Debate (2nd edition, 2015) shows that outcomes differ substantially between clinicians using the same approach. For anxiety and depression specifically, Hofmann and colleagues' review of 106 meta-analyses (2012) documents large, consistent effects for structured cognitive-behavioral treatment.

So the question to ask is whether this person has a method with evidence behind it, and whether they apply it to what is actually driving your problem. Liking them helps that work. It does not replace it.

What Questions Should You Ask a Therapist in Singapore Before Booking?

1. What is your specialization, and who certified it?

The six questions to ask a therapist in Singapore before booking are: what the specialization is and who certified it; what happens in the first sessions; how progress will be measured; whether the clinician works with the body as well as the conversation; whether the method is written down; and what the fee buys. Start with the first. "I work with anxiety, depression, trauma, relationships, self-esteem and life transitions" is a list, not a specialization. A specialist names one or two areas and the institution that trained them in it. Ask for both.

2. What happens in the first four sessions?

A clinician who treats mechanisms begins with assessment: symptoms, history, patterns, and physiology, including sleep, energy, and the body's stress response. If the answer is "we start talking and see where it goes," you are being offered open-ended support, which has its place, but is not treatment for a defined problem.

3. How will we know it is working?

Structured therapy measures. Validated instruments at intake and at intervals (the PHQ-9 for depression, the GAD-7 for anxiety), plus observable targets: hours slept, time to recover after a trigger, tasks no longer avoided. Lambert and Shimokawa (2011) showed that routinely monitoring outcomes and feeding them back into treatment improves results and cuts the number of clients who deteriorate. A therapist who cannot tell you how progress will be measured has no way to know whether to change course.

4. Do you work with the body, or only with the conversation?

Burnout, anxiety, and low mood run on a stress system. Bruce McEwen's concept of allostatic load (1998) describes what sustained activation does to that system over months: cortisol rhythm, sleep architecture, heart rate variability, immune function. A clinician who understands this treats the physiology as part of the plan, not as a referral to someone else. Ask how they address sleep and nervous system regulation. Listen for mechanism, not for "relaxation."

5. What is your method, and is it written down?

"Integrative" and "eclectic" often mean "whatever comes up." A method you can read, in a manual, a published protocol, or a page on the therapist's own site, means the clinician has committed to a sequence that can be examined and compared with the evidence. It also means you can decide before you book whether the approach makes sense to you.

6. What does a session cost, and what does the price buy?

Private psychotherapy in Singapore runs between S$150 and S$350 a session, with established practices clustering in the upper half; the full breakdown is in the cost guide. Price alone separates nothing. A S$350 open-ended conversation is expensive; a S$150 structured treatment with a defined end is not. What you are paying for is the five answers above. One Singapore-specific detail: if you plan to claim through a corporate plan or an employee assistance programme, ask the insurer whether the clinician's title matters to them, because in Singapore it frequently does, and that can decide the choice before any clinical criterion is applied. If moving abroad is part of your situation, the relocation dimension belongs in the assessment too.

The Singapore therapist checklist

Seven quick checks, one line each, drawn from the six questions above. A clinician who passes most of them is a health professional with a method; one who passes only the first is a safe listener, which may or may not be what you need.

  • Registered or verifiably trained at postgraduate level, with supervised clinical hours.
  • Names a specialization and the institution that certified it.
  • Describes an assessment phase before intervention begins.
  • Can say how progress will be measured, with instruments and observable targets.
  • Addresses sleep and the stress response as part of the plan.
  • Works from a method you can read before you book.
  • States the fee and what it buys, with no pressure to commit before the first conversation.

What Should Happen in Your First Session With a Psychotherapist?

A serious first session with a psychotherapist in Singapore, online or in a room, does three things.

First, assessment. A structured review of symptoms, history, patterns, and physiology: when the sleep changed, what the energy does across a week, what the body does under load. The purpose is to identify what is maintaining the difficulty, which is rarely the most visible symptom. In my practice this takes the first sessions and uses validated instruments alongside clinical interview.

Second, formulation. The assessment produces a working picture of the mechanism: a stress system that no longer switches off, a depletion loop, an avoidance pattern that has consolidated. The formulation is shared with you in plain terms. If you cannot explain, after the first sessions, why your clinician thinks this is happening, something is missing.

Third, a plan with targets. Which mechanism is addressed first and why, what will be done in session and between sessions, and what observable change is expected by when. Progress is then tracked against those targets, and the plan is adjusted when the data says so. This is the structure behind anxiety therapy and burnout therapy at Baseline Psychotherapy, and the intervention is designed case by case; the Baseline Psychotherapy Protocol, the process-based method published by J.R. Hernandez, is the base from which each one is built. For clients based in Singapore, sessions run online on local evening hours, in English or Spanish.

For a validated depression score before your first call, the PHQ-9 is here, free and private.

Choosing a psychotherapist in Singapore is not a search for the person you would most enjoy talking to. It is a search for a health professional who can identify what is maintaining your problem and change it with a method that has evidence behind it. The connection matters, and the research gives it roughly eight percent of the result. The rest is shared between the method, its fit to the mechanism, the clinician's competence, and your own life; the first three are what you can verify before you book.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a referral to see a psychotherapist in Singapore?

No. Private psychotherapy in Singapore does not require a doctor's referral; you contact the clinician directly and book. A referral matters only if you intend to claim through an insurance plan that requires one, or if you are being seen in the public system through a polyclinic or hospital department. For a private online practice, the first step is simply a conversation, and in this practice that conversation is free and lasts fifteen minutes.

Is online psychotherapy from Singapore as effective as going to a clinic?

For the conditions most people seek help for, yes. The meta-analysis by Carlbring and colleagues (2018), pooling twenty randomized trials, found internet-delivered cognitive-behavioral therapy equivalent to face-to-face treatment for anxiety, depression, and related conditions. What the format changes is logistics: for professionals in Singapore with full calendars, a session that fits between meetings, on local evening hours, is often the difference between treatment happening and not happening.

What is the difference between a psychotherapist and a counsellor in Singapore?

Scope and depth of training. Counselling in Singapore is typically a postgraduate qualification focused on supportive, shorter-term, problem-centered work, with voluntary registration through the SAC. Psychotherapy is the structured treatment of emotional and behavioral patterns using a defined method, and the title is not statutorily protected, so the clinician's actual training is what defines it. A psychotherapist with medical and neuroscience training, and a specialization certified by a body such as the APA, is working at a different level of the problem than a counsellor trained to listen and support, and both are legitimate; they are simply not interchangeable.

Is there psychotherapy in Spanish in Singapore?

Yes, though it is rare. Most Spanish-speaking professionals in Singapore end up working in their second language, which is fine for a business meeting and a real limitation when describing what wakes you at four in the morning: emotional vocabulary lives in the language in which the emotion was first learned. This practice runs sessions in Spanish, English, or both, online, on Singapore time. A free 15-minute consultation is enough to check the fit in whichever language you think in.
