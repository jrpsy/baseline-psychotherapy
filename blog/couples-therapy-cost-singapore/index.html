How Much Does Couples Therapy Cost in Singapore?
A Therapist's Guide

By J.R. Hernandez, Psychotherapist · November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Private couples therapy in Singapore costs between S$180 and S$350 per session in most practices. Premium clinics charge S$280 to S$675 for sessions of 75 to 100 minutes, and non-profit centres offer means-tested sessions from about S$40 to S$170. Four things explain the spread: how long the session is, how the therapist was trained, whether a clinic or an independent practitioner is billing, and whether the work happens in a room or online. I am a bilingual psychotherapist based in Singapore, working within Integrative Behavioral Couples Therapy, and my own couples sessions are 90 minutes at $170 USD, about S$220, online, in English, Spanish or both. This guide sets out what the market charges, why, and how to decide what is worth paying for.

How Much Does Couples Therapy Cost in Singapore per Session?

At the subsidised end, the Counselling and Care Centre, a non-profit, charges S$200 per hour at full fee and S$40 to S$170 after a means test for households earning S$9,500 a month or less. Platform-based counsellors such as TYHO start from about S$153 for a 60-minute session.

In private practice, which is where most expats and professionals end up, published rates in 2026 cluster between S$220 and S$300. Heal Counselling charges from S$200 for 60 minutes and from S$300 for 90. The Mind Collected charges S$280 for an 80-minute couples session, S$320 for the intake. Psychology Blossom starts couples work at S$220 for 50 to 60 minutes and S$280 for an 80-minute first session. Sofia Wellness Clinic lists S$280 to S$300 for 75 minutes.

At the premium end, clinic founders and senior clinical psychologists charge S$350 to S$675 per session, usually for 90 to 100 minutes. The highest published figure I found in Singapore is S$675 for 100 minutes.

Note the unit before comparing. A S$220 session of 50 minutes and a S$300 session of 90 minutes cost almost the same per minute, and the longer one is the better buy for a couple, for reasons I explain below.

Why Is Couples Therapy More Expensive Than Individual Therapy?

Three reasons, and they are legitimate.

The session is longer. Individual therapy fits in 50 to 60 minutes. Couples work needs 75 to 90, because two histories have to be heard before anything can be worked on, and because the most useful moments in couples therapy happen when a real exchange between partners is slowed down and examined in the room. That cannot be done in 50 minutes without cutting someone off.

The training is separate. Working with two people who are, at least partly, in conflict with each other is a different skill from working with one person who wants help. Couples therapists train in a specific model, and the evidence-based ones (the Gottman Method, Emotionally Focused Therapy, Integrative Behavioral Couples Therapy) each take years to learn properly. You are paying for that, and you should ask which one you are paying for.

And almost nobody reimburses it. Individual therapy is sometimes covered by international health insurance once a diagnosis is on file. Couples therapy almost never is, so the full fee comes out of the couple's pocket, which makes practices price it with care rather than with insurer tables in mind.

Does Insurance Cover Couples Therapy in Singapore?

Assume it does not, and treat anything else as a bonus. The international health plans most expats in Singapore hold (Cigna, Bupa, Allianz, AXA, Aetna) typically cover outpatient mental health for an individual with a clinical diagnosis, within an annual cap, and typically exclude relationship or marriage counselling by name. Local integrated shield plans do not cover it either. Some employers include a handful of counselling sessions through an employee assistance programme, and those can be used for couples work if the programme allows it; read the terms rather than the brochure.

What I would not do is ask a therapist to bill a couples session as individual treatment to get it reimbursed. It is insurance fraud, it is a bad start to a therapy that depends on honesty, and any clinician who agrees to it is telling you something about how they practise.

Which Approach Are You Paying For?

The name of the model matters more than the name of the clinic, because it determines what the sessions will actually look like.

The Gottman Method

Built on John Gottman's decades of observational research on what distinguishes couples who stay together from couples who do not. Strong on assessment and on concrete communication skills; sessions tend to be structured and educational. Common in Singapore.

Emotionally Focused Therapy

Sue Johnson's attachment-based model. Works on the emotional cycle underneath the arguments, on the premise that most conflict is a protest against disconnection. Well supported by research, and the most widely offered approach in Singapore private practice.

Integrative Behavioral Couples Therapy

This is the model I work with. Developed by Andrew Christensen and Neil Jacobson in the 1990s, Integrative Behavioral Couples Therapy, or IBCT, combines two things that older behavioural approaches kept separate: deliberate change, where partners learn to do something differently, and acceptance, where partners learn to stop fighting the differences that are not going to change. The large randomised trial by Christensen and colleagues, with a five-year follow-up published in 2010, found that about two thirds of IBCT couples were reliably improved at two years and about half were still improved at five, which is unusual durability for couples work.

I use it with international couples for a specific reason. IBCT treats the differences between two partners as the material of the therapy, not as the problem to be eliminated. When one partner grew up in Buenos Aires and the other in Surrey, or one in Manila and the other in Madrid, a great deal of what looks like incompatibility is two cultures running on different defaults about directness, money, family, and how affection is shown. A model built around understanding differences before changing them fits those couples better than one built around teaching both partners a single correct script.

What Does Bilingual, Cross-Cultural Couples Therapy Change, and What Does It Cost?

Most couples who contact me from Singapore share at least one of two features: the partners do not share a first language, or they do not share a first culture. Often both.

In a monolingual couples session, the partner working in a second language is always slightly behind. They find the word a beat late, they soften what they meant because the precise version did not come, and they lose arguments they would have won at home. Over years, that asymmetry becomes a pattern neither partner can name: one of them is always more articulate, and therefore always a little more right. I run sessions in English, Spanish or both, moving between them as needed, so that each partner can say the difficult thing in the language where they actually feel it and the other partner hears it accurately. For couples where one partner thinks in Spanish and the other argues in English, this is not a convenience. It is the difference between a therapy that reaches the problem and one that reaches a translation of it.

Culture works the same way, more quietly. What one partner experiences as coldness is, in the other's family, ordinary restraint. What one calls interference from in-laws is, to the other, what family is for. I have lived in six countries on three continents, and in my own relationship I am the partner from somewhere else, so I know those conversations from the inside. The clinical work is to name which conflicts are about the relationship and which are about two cultures colliding, because they are treated differently.

What it costs: a 90-minute couples session is $170 USD, about S$220, online, with both partners wherever they are, including partners in different cities. There is no intake surcharge and no package to buy. Set against the rates above, that is below the private median in Singapore, with more time per session than most practices offer. Singapore residents can ask about the local resident rate in their first message.

The Singapore couples therapy fee checklist

Before you book with anyone, make sure the fee buys you these six things.

  • A named model (Gottman, EFT, IBCT or another evidence-based approach), not "an integrative style".
  • Sessions of at least 75 minutes, so that both partners are heard in the same hour.
  • A fee published before you write, with the session length next to it.
  • Language: the therapist works in a language both of you can argue in, or in both of yours.
  • An assessment phase, usually two to four sessions, before any technique is applied.
  • A way to tell whether it is working: fewer escalations, faster repair, specific agreements that hold.

How Many Sessions Should a Couple Budget For?

In the research trials, IBCT ran to around 26 sessions, which is more than most couples outside a study will do. In practice, with a clear formulation and weekly or fortnightly sessions, most couples I work with see measurable change within 12 to 16 sessions, over three to four months. Measurable means things you can count, not things you feel: how often an argument escalates past the point of repair, how long it takes you to come back to each other afterwards, and whether the specific agreements you made in session are still standing a month later.

The arithmetic, then. Twelve sessions at S$220 is about S$2,640. Twelve at the premium end is S$4,200 to S$8,000. Twelve at a subsidised centre, if you qualify, is under S$2,000. None of those is the real cost of the problem. The real cost is the one nobody puts on a receipt: the months of the same argument, the holiday spent apart in the same room.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is online couples therapy cheaper than in-person therapy in Singapore?

Usually, yes. Practices without a consulting room to pay for price below clinic rates for the same session length, and online sessions remove the problem of getting two busy people into the same building at the same hour. For couples living in different cities, online is the only format that works at all.

Do both partners pay, or is it one fee per session?

One fee per session, for the couple. There is no per-person charge and no supplement for the first session in my practice. How the couple splits it is their decision, and sometimes a useful conversation in itself.

What if we do not share a first language?

Sessions run in English, Spanish or both. Each partner speaks in the language where they actually feel what they are saying, and I move between the two so that nothing important is lost in a translation made under pressure. If neither of your languages is English or Spanish, I am not the right therapist for you and will say so in the first conversation.

Can couples therapy start with only one partner?

The first conversation can; many couples begin because one partner reaches out first. The therapy itself needs both. In IBCT the assessment phase includes one individual session with each partner, so there is a natural place for each of you to speak alone before the joint work starts.

The expensive part of couples therapy is rarely the session. It is the year a couple spends paying for not having one.
